# A Step-Like Transition Underlying the Life and Death of Galaxies

</div>
<div id="authors" markdown="1">

Zhicheng He, Enci Wang, Luis C. Ho, Yingjie Peng, Huiyuan Wang, Yong Shi , Tao Wang, Yongquan Xue et al.

</div>
<div id="abstract" markdown="1">

**Abstract:** Galaxy quenching is a complex and fundamental problem in astrophysics, yet a simple, unified framework connecting diverse quenching mechanisms remains lacking. Here, we derive a step-function quenching response from the fundamental principle of gas mass conservation, governed by the competition between gas-supply and gas-depletion timescales. This response also enables the gas-supply timescale to be inferred from the observed quenched fraction. Applying this method to approximately 350,000 galaxies, we find a characteristic gas-supply timescale of 1 Gyr, reveal distinct inside-out and outside-in quenching signatures associated with supermassive black holes and dark matter halos, respectively, and identify galaxies with intermediate stellar surface mass densities as occupying a critical state for quenching and rejuvenation. Our work establishes a unified physical framework in which a simple step-function response captures how diverse physical processes regulate galaxy quenching and the resumption of star formation through their effects on the competition between gas-supply and gas-depletion timescales.

**Figure 1. -** Supply-timescale distributions inferred from the quenched fraction for a sample of 347,182 low-redshift galaxies. (a,b) Quenched fraction as a function of stellar surface density for central and satellite galaxies, respectively. **Figure 2. -** The characteristic supply timescale increases with both $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$ and halo mass, while its distribution narrows with increasing $M_{\rm BH}/M_*$ but broadens with halo mass. (a--c) Quenched fraction as a function of stellar surface density for galaxies divided into three bins according to their black hole-to-stellar mass ratios ($M_{\rm BH}/M_*$). The decline of the quenched fraction at high stellar surface density also becomes progressively weaker. (d--f) Same as panels (a--c), but for galaxies divided into three bins of dark matter halo mass. Increasing halo mass shifts the supply-timescale distribution toward larger values and broadens the distribution. The decrease of the quenched fraction at high stellar surface density becomes slightly weaker with increasing halo mass. (*fig2*)

**Figure 3. -** Galaxy quenching is most sensitive to black-hole and halo regulation at intermediate stellar surface densities. (a) Quenched fraction as a function of black hole-to-stellar mass ratio ($M_{\rm BH}/M_*$) in three stellar surface density regimes: low, intermediate, and high $\Sigma_*$. The difference between the high- and low-$\Sigma_*$ quenched fractions shows a weak decreasing trend with increasing halo mass. (*fig3*)

**Figure 4. -** An observationally motivated picture for galaxy quenching. At low and intermediate $\Sigma_*$, star formation is spatially extended and gas supply is primarily regulated by cooling from the hot circumgalactic halo, making the supply timescale nearly independent of $\Sigma_*$. At high $\Sigma_*$, star formation and cold gas become increasingly concentrated toward the central region, causing the supply timescale to decrease rapidly. Together with the declining depletion timescale, this naturally makes the two timescales most comparable at intermediate $\Sigma_*$ (critical state marked by gray shading), where quenching is most sensitive to changes in the supply timescale. Because SMBH feedback acts most efficiently on centrally concentrated gas, SMBH preferentially increases the supply timescale of high-$\Sigma_*$ galaxies, whereas dark matter halos exert a relatively stronger influence on low-$\Sigma_*$ galaxies. (*fig4*)

**Figure 5. -** Stellar mass versus black hole mass for the GSWLC--UPenn--Yang--NYU cross-matched galaxy sample. **Figure 8. -** Dependence of the supply-timescale distribution on morphology-dependent black hole mass estimates. Same as Fig. fig2(a--c), but using morphology-dependent black hole mass estimates instead of the unified scaling relation greene2020.
